(ii) Estimate expected cash expenditures.

After identifying cash receipts, this
step lists all expected expenditures
of the business during the budgeting
period. For instance, a retail shop
owner should include rent, electricity
bills, water bills, transport costs and
acquisition of non-current assets in
the budget. This ensures all necessary
cash expenditures are accounted for,
minimising the risk of unexpected
cash shortages. Non-cash items,
such as depreciation and provision
for doubtful debts are excluded in
cash budget preparations.

(iii) Determine the budget period.

Depending on the financial needs,
a cash budget should be prepared
for a specific period, such as daily,
weekly, or monthly. For example,
a food vendor may prepare a daily
cash budget to track cash receipts
from selling food and control

spending on ingredients. A well-
defined time frame helps to track
financial performance and make
timely adjustments.

(iv) Calculate

the

expected

cash

balance
. The cash balance is obtained
by subtracting total disbursements
from

total

cash

receipts.

For

example, if a poultry farmer expects
to collect TZS 5,000,000 from sales
and plans to spend TZS 400,000 on
feed, vaccines and transport, they
will have a remaining cash balance
of TZS 4,600,000. This step enables
the farmer to determine whether
there will be enough cash to cover
expenditures or adjustments are
needed.

(v) Monitor and adjust the budget
frequently
. A cash budget should
be reviewed often to compare actual
cash flow with budgeted amounts
and make necessary changes. For
instance, if a retail shop owner finds
that electricity costs are higher than
expected, they may adjust their
spending on less urgent expenses.

Regular monitoring helps to improve
financial management and avoid
unnecessary cash shortages.
